2. Cycle Position

The macro board prints the levels: fed funds 3.63%, core CPI 2.5%, unemployment 4.1%, payrolls −23k. The narrative here is not the levels but what they imply for the capital cycle our themes depend on.

The inflation trend is gently favourable — core CPI has fallen 0.4pp over twelve months, headline down 0.2pp — which gives the Fed room to cut if labour weakens further. One negative payroll print is not a trend, but it is a change in sign after months of positive but declining numbers, and Druckenmiller’s lens asks whether this is the start of an inflection. It is too early to call, but the store should note that a further weakening would change the “immaculate disinflation” assumption quietly embedded in the capex‑boom narrative.

Three layers of the buildout are financed differently, and the interest‑rate channel hits each differently. Hyperscaler capex is overwhelmingly cash‑flow‑funded (Dalio), so the immediate transmission is through demand for cloud services during a slowdown, not through financing costs. Grid‑connected generation, the layer where many of our energy‑spending names sit, is project‑financed; a sustained 3.63% rate raises the hurdle for new assets that have not yet locked in PPAs. Regulated utilities, the third group, are less exposed because allowed returns are set in proceedings that lag policy rates. The correct statement is therefore not “rates are a risk to the theme” but “rates are a risk concentrated in the merchant‑generation and project‑finance layers, and the utilities and hyperscalers largely pass through.”

The stores’ most important macro‑to‑equity link right now is the one the profit‑pool mapping highlights: if economic softening reduces advertising and enterprise consumption of AI services, that hits the layer that nobody in our universe directly owns (Application layer) but indirectly determines the order book for everyone below it. That softening has not appeared in any order data yet, but the payrolls print is the earliest possible straw in the wind.

3. Structural Change Radar

Artificial Intelligence

Age of Electricity. The Q2 evidence across the chain is overwhelming on demand: GE Vernova’s gas equipment backlog plus reservations rose to 116 GW, orders +88% organic; Eaton’s twelve‑month rolling electrical orders +41%; Quanta’s outlook raised across all metrics; Powell Industries booked $490M of orders at 1.7× book‑to‑bill, with a single data‑centre order above $400M after quarter‑end. Constellation signed 920 MW of new PPAs at an 18.5‑year average duration. Against this, the EIA’s Annual Energy Outlook still projects US electricity demand growth of 0.9–1.6% p.a., a multi‑decade view that treats the current acceleration as a demand‑pull that may not persist at recent rates. The theme is directionally confirmed; the open question is how much of the current order surge is pulling forward a capital cycle that will eventually plateau, and how much represents a permanent step‑change in the load curve.

For the utility sub‑theme, the NextEra‑Dominion merger is proceeding through regulatory filings, with $2.25B of shareholder‑funded bill credits offered to Dominion customers — a concrete pricing of the political constraint on rate‑base growth. A regulated utility does not grow with load; it grows with allowed return on invested capital, and the bill credit models the trade‑off.

AI Physical Infrastructure. The same Q2 filings apply. The standout risk is Vertiv’s decision to not publish orders or backlog in its Q2 earnings release, after those very metrics were flagged as the principal monitoring variable in the W29 thesis. Revenue of $3.27B (+24%) and adjusted operating margin of 22.6% (+410bp) are good results, but the missing data removes the forward visibility that allowed one to distinguish a genuine multi‑year buildout from a delivery‑schedule flush. The store will read the Vertiv 10‑Q and any supplemental deck before drawing a firm conclusion, but the omission is a negative signal in an otherwise supportive quarter.

AI profit pool. The iCapital benchmark (July 2026) is frozen as the reference point; it attributes 29.7¢ of profit per AI dollar to hyperscaler cloud and 13.0¢ to commercial chips, two layers in which we hold zero names. The pool map shows our heaviest concentration is in the equipment‑profit layer (16 names, 3.8¢ total) and the energy‑spending flow (19 names, treated as cost with no profit box). This has not changed since the last report, but the asymmetry has never been clearer: we think we are buying the beneficiaries of the buildout, and the biggest single monetisation — half of all measured profit — sits entirely outside our coverage. Chanos asks who is financing the demand: the hyperscalers are, and we own none of them. This is the structural finding that should dominate our research priorities.

High‑speed connectivity. No new primary evidence this period. Credo’s 10‑K concentration risk (two customers, 81% of revenue) remains unresolved. The ASIC‑versus‑merchant chip dynamic is frozen; Broadcom’s custom silicon lies in a layer that is 1.1¢ of profit, not 13.0¢, and a shift of share into that layer shrinks the aggregate pool.

Advanced Nuclear Enablers. The policy environment remains supportive (NRC Part 53 pathway, presidential‑level minerals determinations) but no monitored asset has produced commercial‑scale data this period. Fuel‑cycle names are tracked as options, not as theses.

Overall. The section’s thesis strengthened on the demand evidence, but the missing forecasting data from Vertiv introduces a note of caution. The profit‑pool concentration in untracked layers remains the most important open question in the entire store.

Digital Assets

Stablecoin Payment Rails. Total fiat‑backed supply grew 0.44% week‑over‑week to $284.6B; supply on payment‑oriented chains was flat (−0.3%) at $117.4B. USDC’s share ticked up to 23.9%, continuing a very gradual institutional shift. The float grows, but at a rate that suggests adoption is steady rather than accelerating. None of this triggers a thesis change, and we still hold zero names.

Agentic Commerce. The Cloudflare Q2 data (now verified against primary sources) tells a more nuanced story than the social‑media thread that initially flagged it. The 1,700% growth in daily AI agent requests is real but starts from a low base. The headline “more than 50% of traffic is non‑human” is correct, but the company does not break out what portion is AI agents versus bots, crawlers and scrapers; the agent‑specific number the thesis needs is unknown. Management’s estimate that 1‑10% of requests could be monetised is a wide range, and the Monetisation Gateway is waitlist‑only with no disclosed pricing.

The critical structural finding (Druckenmiller calls it the inflection no one is noticing) is that x402 settlement now batches transactions off‑chain. This decouples agent request volume from on‑chain settlement volume by design, so the thesis that “agent growth drives settlement‑layer fees” fails. The edge‑distribution layer (Cloudflare, browser owners) may capture value, but the settlement layer is seeing fewer transactions than the raw traffic numbers imply. We have zero tracked names in any digital‑assets layer, and that remains the correct position: the theme is real and early, but every identifiable investment conclusion is premature.

Industrial Frontier. Rocket Lab filed the S‑4 for the Iridium merger, which is a necessary administrative step but not a value‑creating event; the financing and integration risks remain. MP Materials’ Q2 is filed but not yet reviewed. No thesis movement in either.

The panel’s questions are answered within the numbered sections where they bear, not in a separate block. No seat changed a sentence into a finding, but each added a question the store cannot yet answer; those questions are carried into the Research Queue.

- Chanos (forensic): asked whether anyone in the chain books revenue from a party it also funded. The hyperscalers finance their own demand and we do not own them; this is not circular revenue but a concentration risk. He also asked about depreciation schedule or capitalisation policy changes amid a capex boom; the store holds no asset‑level data on this, and it is added to the research backlog for the equipment names. The missing Vertiv orders also raise his usual question: what stopped being said.
- Dalio (macro): asked who is borrowing to fund this spending and on what terms. The answer for hyperscalers is “from operating cash flow,” but for merchant generation and some equipment capacity expansion, the answer is “project finance at current policy rates.” The store does not yet track the debt‑funding share of the Age of Electricity buildout; that is added to the Queue.
- Damodaran (valuation): asked which valuation input each part of the story changes. The growth estimates file provides revenue CAGR ranges, but no market‑implied valuation multiples have been attached to the candidate names. The store therefore has a narrative with insufficient numeric translation. This is a process gap, not a one‑week finding. He also noted that the addressable market (data‑centre load growth) is counted many times across names without the TAM being sized once; that work belongs in the section research files.
- Druckenmiller (inflection): asked which sentence names an inflection rather than a state. The payrolls negative print is the only inflection candidate, but it is one month and insufficient. The missing Vertiv orders could become an inflection if it turns into a permanent disappearance. The more important question — “if this thesis is right, what must be bought in the next six months?” — is already answered by the order books: transformers, switchgear, gas turbines, and that is happening. So the evidence supports the “state of demand” interpretation rather than an inflection.

One committee question could not be answered from the store and will be added to the Queue: Chanos’ depreciation/capitalisation policy check for gearbox, switchgear and cooling names, which would require reading each recent 10‑K’s accounting policies. This is not a one‑week task.
