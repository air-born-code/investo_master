2. Cycle Position

The macro regime is drifting toward a soft landing. Core CPI (CPILFESL) fell to 2.4% year over year in August, its lowest since early‑2025, while headline CPI nudged down to 3.4%. The unemployment rate held at 4.1%, and nonfarm payrolls rebounded to +162k after July’s −23k, suggesting that the prior month was noise rather than a trend break. The effective federal funds rate remains at 3.63%, unchanged for four months.

For the store’s themes, the relevant shift is in the trajectory of rate expectations: core disinflation removes one obstacle to eventual easing, but the funds rate is still elevated and the forward curve is not yet pricing aggressive cuts. That matters because the AI buildout—generation, grid, data centres—is long-duration and capital-intensive, so its valuations are sensitive to the discount rate even before any earnings change. The current level of 3.63% is well below the 5.33% peak of 2023, but it is not obviously stimulative; the market appears to be projecting a “higher for longer” plateau.

George Soros’s reflexivity lens asks: which part of this theme is being created by belief in the theme? The AI capex cycle has a self‑reinforcing character: rising hyperscaler spending begets more demand for chips, which pulls in more electricity and creates more infrastructure orders, which then supports the narrative of a structural demand shift. The funding that sustains the cycle—17.3 cents of external money per dollar of AI spend, according to the iCapital chain exhibit—is itself a bet on future returns that are not yet visible. If that belief weakens (e.g., because corporate AI adoption disappoints or model‑layer losses persist), the entire chain from foundation models down could contract, and the electricity “shortage” might evaporate faster than the project pipeline implies. No data this week makes such a reversal likely, but we must keep tracking the layers that depend on external funding, not just the ones building physical plant.

For the sectors we track, the electricity equipment and generation layers are still early in their own cycles—GE Vernova’s order book stretches years, and transformer lead times remain extreme—but the reflexivity caution applies equally: the orders that look like structural demand today could prove to be over‑ordering if the ultimate compute customer doesn’t materialise. We do not have a kill signal, only a reminder that the cycle position is sensitive to sentiment in the layer above ours.

Age of Electricity. No new primary evidence this week. The core thesis—that data‑centre load growth is generating a genuine electricity‑demand acceleration—remains supported by the IEA’s 2026 forecast (src‑2026‑w32‑iea‑electricity‑2026) and by the Q2 results already in the store. However, the reflexivity question applies here: if hyperscaler capex were to decelerate because AI models fail to generate enterprise returns, the load forecasts that justify utility capital plans and merchant generator valuations would be revised downward. The same risk applies to every transformer and switchgear name whose backlog is built on data‑centre orders.

Rick Rule’s question about permitting is acute for the electricity theme. The IEA and EIA documents describe demand, but they say nothing about the decade‑long lead times for transmission corridors, generator interconnection, and transformer manufacturing expansion. For a company like GE Vernova, which plans to expand gas turbine output from 20 GW annualised to 30 GW by 2030, the speed‑limiting factor is factory floor‑space and the ability to hire and train the skilled workforce—permits in the classic extractive sense are not the bottleneck, but the concept of a physical buildout constrained by time and regulation still applies. We lack any data in the store on the permit or construction timelines for the specific turbine plants, but the general observation is that capacity additions do not happen overnight, which lends credibility to the scarcity‑rent thesis.

Nick Sleep’s destination analysis asks what GE Vernova looks like at maturity. The current narrative is that it becomes a high‑margin, services‑heavy energy infrastructure company, akin to the old GE Power but without the conglomerate drag. The Q2 data show a 176 B backlog and a growing services annuity, but the services share of revenue is not broken out, and the valuation at $264 B already credits a long runway. We cannot build a destination model from the present store; the required segmentation data is still buried in unreviewed 10‑Qs.
