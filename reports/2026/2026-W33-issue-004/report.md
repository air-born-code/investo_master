### How the regime reaches equity prices

Transmission mechanisms, not forecasts. Each states the channel through which a
series is capable of moving prices; none asserts that it will, or in which direction.

**Fed funds.** Reaches equities through the discount rate rather than through earnings. A higher rate lowers the present value of distant cash flow more than near cash flow, so long-duration and capital-intensive businesses reprice hardest. For this store's themes the second channel matters more: generation, grid and data-centre capacity is project-financed, so the policy rate is an input cost to the buildout itself, not only a valuation multiplier.

*Read with care:* The effective rate is a monthly average of realised overnight trading, so it lags the target range decision by up to a month. Read the FOMC statement for policy intent, not this series.

**Headline CPI.** Two channels. Through policy: sustained headline inflation removes the option of rate cuts that equity valuations tend to price in advance. Through the income statement: a company growing revenue at the inflation rate is growing units at zero, so inflation determines how much reported growth is actually growth. Pricing power is the variable that decides which of a theme's companies keep the difference.

*Read with care:* Headline includes food and energy and is therefore the noisier series, but it is the one that sets wage expectations and household behaviour. It should not be dismissed as noise merely because core is smoother.

**Core CPI.** The dominant channel is policy expectations. Because core is the series the committee weights, a change in core moves the forward rate curve, and the curve moves equity multiples within days — long before any company's earnings change. That is why a core print can reprice an entire theme in a week without a single fact about any business in it having changed.

*Read with care:* Core excludes food and energy, which is a deliberate signal-extraction choice, not a claim those costs do not matter. Shelter is a large and heavily lagged component, so core turns later than the underlying pressure does.

**Unemployment.** Reaches equities through both mandates at once, in opposite directions. A rising unemployment rate weakens demand for cyclical goods and therefore earnings, while simultaneously pulling policy easier and therefore supporting multiples. Which effect dominates is an empirical question about the specific episode, not something that can be asserted in advance. For structurally-driven capital spending the relevant test is whether committed capacity plans survive a labour downturn, and that is answered by order books, not by this series.

*Read with care:* The rate can fall for a good reason (hiring) or a bad one (people leaving the labour force). The level alone cannot distinguish them; the participation rate and the household-survey detail can.

**Payrolls.** Moves rate expectations faster than it moves earnings. A surprising print is a repricing event, not an earnings event: nothing about any company's cash flow changed on the morning of the release. The correct response to a payroll surprise is therefore to ask whether it changes the policy path, and separately whether it changes any tracked business — usually the first and not the second.

*Read with care:* Heavily revised, frequently by more than the original surprise. A single month is close to meaningless and a negative print can be revised positive. Three-month averages carry information; one print does not.

A macro reading is context for underwriting, never a trigger for action. Nothing
here is a view on any tracked company.

## 2. Cycle Position

Interpretation only; the board above carries the levels.

**Core inflation has now fallen for three consecutive months on the store's own history** (`macro_history.csv`: core CPI three-month change −0.20pp, twelve-month change −0.40pp) while the policy rate has been essentially static over the same window (three-month change −0.01pp). That combination is what a market prices as "cuts are coming." It is also what a market has *already* priced, which is the more useful observation: the watchlist multiples in `weekly_metrics.csv` are being carried against an easing path that has not yet appeared in the funds rate.

The labour series is where the trajectory matters more than the level. Payrolls printed −23k, the three-month mean is +20k and the twelve-month mean is +26k. A negative print alone is close to meaningless — `macro_series.csv` is explicit that this series is heavily revised and that one month carries no information. What is not noise is that the twelve-month mean and the three-month mean have converged at a low level. Labour demand is not deteriorating fast; it has flattened.

For this store's themes the transmission is the second channel named in `macro_series.csv` for FEDFUNDS, not the first: generation, grid and data-centre capacity is project-financed, so the policy rate is an *input cost to the buildout*, not only a discount rate. A softer rate path is therefore doubly favourable to the electricity and construction layers — cheaper project debt and higher present value of long-dated cash flow at once. That is precisely the reason to be suspicious: a theme that benefits twice from the same macro move is a theme whose current price may be doing more macro work than fundamental work.

**Where expectations look like extrapolation.** The equipment and grid names are priced against order books that are themselves priced against a specific capital-spending path. `src-2026-w32-hyperscaler-runrate` records the arithmetic: Q1 2026 combined capex of $129.8B annualises to $519B against a reported full-year aggregate near $725B. Reaching the headline requires spending well above the Q1 run-rate through the back half. Amazon's Q2 figure of $54.21B, up 23% sequentially (src-2026-w32-amzn-capex-q2), is directionally consistent with that step-up and is the only Q2 datapoint filed among the four. One name is not four. Until the remaining three Q2 filings are read, the widely-cited aggregate remains a *forward commitment* rather than an established rate, and the order books that depend on it are contingent on a commitment rather than on a trend.

**The counter-cyclical exposure in the store is thinner than it looks.** Regulated rate-base names (NEE, AEP, DUK, SO, EXC, D) earn an allowed return on invested capital rather than a price, which insulates earnings from the cycle — but `growth_estimates.csv` names the binding constraint for both NEE and AEP as political and regulatory, not economic. The $2.25B of shareholder-funded bill credits in the NextEra–Dominion applications (src-2026-w32-nee-d-merger) is that constraint being priced explicitly. A recession does not remove regulatory risk; it can raise it.

**Cycle-position judgement.** Merchant generation is the layer where cycle position most changes what a valuation means. CEG and VST are re-contracting existing assets into a market the PJM auction shows to be short — the 2027/2028 base residual cleared at the FERC-approved cap of $333.44/MW-day and still fell 6,623 MW short of the reliability requirement (src-2026-w32-pjm-capacity), with the 2028/2029 auction clearing at $325.00/MW-day (src-edgar-000114036126028454, src-edgar-000186827526000080). Clearing at a cap is not a market price; it is an administrative ceiling on a shortage. That is late-cycle scarcity pricing in the capacity market specifically, and `growth_estimates.csv` is right to label VST a "merchant-price-bet" that is "directional both ways."
