How the regime reaches equity prices

Transmission mechanisms, not forecasts. Each states the channel through which a series is capable of moving prices; none asserts that it will, or in which direction.

Fed funds

Reaches equities through the discount rate rather than through earnings. A higher rate lowers the present value of distant cash flow more than near cash flow, so long-duration and capital-intensive businesses reprice hardest. For this store's themes the second channel matters more: generation, grid and data-centre capacity is project-financed, so the policy rate is an input cost to the buildout itself, not only a valuation multiplier.

Read with care: The effective rate is a monthly average of realised overnight trading, so it lags the target range decision by up to a month. Read the FOMC statement for policy intent, not this series.

Headline CPI

Two channels. Through policy: sustained headline inflation removes the option of rate cuts that equity valuations tend to price in advance. Through the income statement: a company growing revenue at the inflation rate is growing units at zero, so inflation determines how much reported growth is actually growth. Pricing power is the variable that decides which of a theme's companies keep the difference.

Read with care: Headline includes food and energy and is therefore the noisier series, but it is the one that sets wage expectations and household behaviour. It should not be dismissed as noise merely because core is smoother.

Core CPI

The dominant channel is policy expectations. Because core is the series the committee weights, a change in core moves the forward rate curve, and the curve moves equity multiples within days — long before any company's earnings change. That is why a core print can reprice an entire theme in a week without a single fact about any business in it having changed.

Read with care: Core excludes food and energy, which is a deliberate signal-extraction choice, not a claim those costs do not matter. Shelter is a large and heavily lagged component, so core turns later than the underlying pressure does.

Unemployment

Reaches equities through both mandates at once, in opposite directions. A rising unemployment rate weakens demand for cyclical goods and therefore earnings, while simultaneously pulling policy easier and therefore supporting multiples. Which effect dominates is an empirical question about the specific episode, not something that can be asserted in advance. For structurally-driven capital spending the relevant test is whether committed capacity plans survive a labour downturn, and that is answered by order books, not by this series.

Read with care: The rate can fall for a good reason (hiring) or a bad one (people leaving the labour force). The level alone cannot distinguish them; the participation rate and the household-survey detail can.

Payrolls

Moves rate expectations faster than it moves earnings. A surprising print is a repricing event, not an earnings event: nothing about any company's cash flow changed on the morning of the release. The correct response to a payroll surprise is therefore to ask whether it changes the policy path, and separately whether it changes any tracked business — usually the first and not the second.

Read with care: Heavily revised, frequently by more than the original surprise. A single month is close to meaningless and a negative print can be revised positive. Three-month averages carry information; one print does not.

A macro reading is context for underwriting, never a trigger for action. Nothing here is a view on any tracked company.

1. Executive Summary

The week’s most consequential change is monetary, not corporate: the effective federal funds rate rose to 3.75% after holding at 3.63% since mid-2026 (src‑2026‑w40‑fred‑fedfunds). This is the first upward move in the series since the Fed paused, and it arrives alongside a soft labour market—payrolls +29k, unemployment 4.2%—and slowing core CPI (2.4% y/y). The macro regime board already displays the levels; what matters here is the direction and what it forces. An upward rate move with core inflation still falling is an unusual sequence, and it directly raises the discount rate on the long‑duration cash flows that the entire AI infrastructure theme depends on (Damodaran, through seat). It also lifts the cost of project finance for the generation, grid, and data‑centre buildout.

2. Cycle Position

The board already shows the data, so the job here is to say what the *trajectory* means.

Three observations together form a pattern that the store has not seen since the tightening cycle ended:

  • The effective federal funds rate moved up (3.63% → 3.75%).
  • Core CPI continued down (2.5% → 2.4%).
  • Payrolls barely positive (+29k) after a negative July, and unemployment ticked up to 4.2%.

A rate increase when the labour market is softening and core inflation is still declining is either a central bank that sees an inflation risk the market does not, or a policy mistake in the making. Either way, the direction of the policy rate has reversed, and that matters more than the level.

Through the lens of this file, the transmission runs first through the discount rate. Every dollar of electricity revenue that arrives in 2035 is worth less today when the risk‑free rate rises. The equipment backlog (GE Vernova $176B, Eaton’s orders +41%) is real, but the *multiple* the market will pay for that backlog adjusts immediately, and the board does not yet carry a W40 price that would let us measure the adjustment (see §10).

Dalio asks, through the seat: who is borrowing to fund the spending this theme depends on, and on what terms? The AI buildout is capital‑intensive at every layer—grid, generation, data‑centre construction. The hyperscalers are largely self‑funding (Q1 2026 capex $129.8B, annualising to $519B, well short of the aggregated $725B full‑year guide). But the generation layer relies heavily on project finance, and those facilities price off the risk‑free curve. A sustained rate rise, even small, lifts the carrying cost of every new turbine, transformer, and transmission line ordered today. The store has no direct evidence yet that capital expenditure plans are being trimmed, but the lever has moved. Druckenmiller’s lens forces the point: this is an inflection in funding conditions, not merely a new macro reading. The store cannot tell today whether the build‑out absorbs it or breaks against it. The question goes into the Research Queue.

The counter‑cyclical exposures in our universe are minimal. Regulated utilities (NextEra, Dominion) earn a return on rate base that is reset periodically, not daily, so their economics are slower to adjust. Merchant generators (Vistra, Constellation) sell into a short capacity market; higher rates do not immediately reduce the scarcity price unless they compress demand, which is not evident. The risk concentration sits in the long‑duration, high‑multiple equipment names where the justification for the multiple is growth that compounds far into the future.

The external‑funding layer (‑17.3c) is the chain’s least durable input. It is not investable, but it is monitored because if private AI funding tightens—as a rising rate environment might cause—the contraction begins at the foundation‑model layer and propagates downward through reduced orders. The store has no current funding data; the series is tracked by private announcements and hyperscaler capex guidance, neither of which were updated this week.

Electricity is a layer of the AI section, not a peer. The returns available to a turbine maker or a switchgear supplier are determined by the chain above them. A higher cost of capital raises the hurdle for every new data‑centre‑backed PPA. If fewer PPAs are signed, the orders that flow to GE Vernova and Powell Industries appear later or smaller. That chain of causation is the reason the AI section sits above electricity; this week’s rate move tests it directly.
