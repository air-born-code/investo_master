Sections omitted this week for lack of material evidence: New Candidates, Scenario Analysis.

2. Cycle Position

The macro board already shows the levels. The interpretation: after the negative July payrolls print (revised to -23k), August’s +162k suggests noise rather than a turn. The labour market remains solid, unemployment steady at 4.1%. Meanwhile, core CPI continues its gradual descent, and the Fed has held rates at 3.63% for over a year. The trajectory is one of slowly easing inflation and a patient central bank. For now, the “no recession, no cut” environment is a reasonable consensus.

Second-level thinking (Howard Marks): the market has already priced a long runway of AI-related capex and a soft landing. Valuations for data-centre equipment and electrical names have expanded sharply. What is not priced in is the possibility that the aggregate $725bn hyperscaler capex guidance for 2026 is not a durable rate, or that the marginal demand for electricity from AI is slower than the most bullish narratives. The EIA’s baseline projection of 0.9%–1.6% annual US electricity demand growth (src-2026-w32-eia-aeo-2026) is a sobering counterpoint to the theme’s 3.6% global demand growth forecast (src-2026-w32-iea-electricity-2026). The former suggests a lot of the current optimism may already be in prices.

Our watchlist is long, but the actual distance to a “high conviction, investable at this price” call is measured in years, not weeks. No change in action posture.

3. Structural Change Radar

3a. Artificial Intelligence

The AI profit pool map (src-2026-w32-icapital-chain) remains our benchmark. No update to the aggregate market cap values in ai_profit_pool.csv this week, so our position is static: 52 names, concentrated in energy spending and equipment profit, zero in hyperscaler cloud, memory, or foundry. The central finding endures: 29.7 cents of every AI dollar flows to the hyperscaler layer we do not track.

Peter Lynch’s question on abstraction applies — the layers can feel like concepts, not products. Below are the concrete goods and services each tracked layer sells:

- Energy spending (19 names) — GE Vernova sells heavy-duty gas turbines and grid transformers; Constellation and Vistra sell electrons from existing nuclear fleets; Quanta Services sells the skilled labour to string transmission lines.
- Equipment profit (16 names) — Vertiv sells UPS units and liquid cooling for server racks; Eaton sells switchgear; Hubbell sells connectors and enclosures.
- Construction spending (3 names) — Quanta and EMCOR sell electrical and mechanical installation.

The external funding layer (17.3 cents of investor capital per dollar of end-user spend) is the chain’s Achilles’ heel. No new data on AI funding rounds or hyperscaler capex cuts this week, but the Q1 run-rate annualised at $519bn (src-2026-w32-hyperscaler-runrate) versus the $725bn full-year target implies a significant ramp in H2, which we will watch.
